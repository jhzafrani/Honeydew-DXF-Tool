1 Getting started

The DXF Tool opens apparel pattern files exported in the AAMA/ASTM (ANSI/AAMA‑DXF) format and lets you view, measure, quality‑check and annotate them right in the browser — nothing to install (though you can, see §8). This section gets you from a blank window to a pattern on screen.

Open the tool

  1. Go to https://honeydew-dxf-tool.netlify.app/ in Chrome, Edge, Safari or Firefox.
  2. When prompted, enter the site password HoneydewTools2026. You’ll land on an empty canvas with a “Drop an apparel DXF here” message.

Load a pattern file

  1. Click 📂 Open in the toolbar (or drag files straight onto the window, or click Choose file… in the drop zone).
  2. Select one or more .dxf pattern files. To get full size information, also select the matching .rul grade‑rule file in the same dialog — the tool pairs it with the DXF automatically.
  3. The pattern draws in the canvas and the left sidebar fills in: the file name and a materials summary at the top, then the list of pieces below.
You can add the .rul later, too: just drag it onto the window while the pattern is open. The sidebar hint updates to “.rul grade table loaded” and size‑aware features (grade nest, size order, grade‑rules export) switch on.
The tool reads AAMA/ASTM apparel DXFs and also plain CAD DXFs (geometry in the ENTITIES section, named layers like CUT / NET / GRAIN). If a file declares its units in the header ($INSUNITS — e.g. inches), the tool scales it correctly and switches the unit selector to match, so measurements read true. Files with no unit header are assumed to be in millimetres.

Move around the pattern

  1. Zoom — scroll the mouse wheel (or pinch on a trackpad). Zoom centers on the cursor, so point at what you want to inspect before scrolling. The zoom step is intentionally gentle for fine control.
  2. Pan — with the ✥ Pan tool active (the default), press and drag anywhere on the canvas.
  3. Fit — click ⤢ Fit (or press F) at any time to frame the current piece or selection; click ▦ All to frame every piece in the file.
  4. The bottom‑left readout always shows the cursor’s x / y position and the current zoom %.

Work with several files at once

Each DXF opens in its own tab across the top of the canvas. Click a tab to switch; click the ✕ on a tab to close it (you’ll be asked to save if it has unsaved changes). The + button opens another file. Having two revisions open side‑by‑side powers the Compare overlay (see §9).

The pattern colors tell you what each line is: white = cut line, blue dashed = sew/seam line, green = grain line, purple = fold/internal, plus smaller marks for seam/internal points, yellow notches and red drill holes. Turn any layer on or off in the Layers panel of the sidebar.

4 Measuring & the ruler tool

There are three ways to measure: a quick straight‑line distance, a seam length that follows a curve, and a draggable guide ruler you can place, rotate and snap. All three read out in whatever unit is selected in the toolbar (mm · cm · inch) and update instantly if you switch units.

Quick measure — straight line

  1. Click 📏 Measure in the toolbar, or press M. The cursor becomes a crosshair.
  2. Click once where you want to start. As you move the mouse a live dashed line follows the cursor.
  3. Click again at the end point. The readout in the bottom‑right shows the straight‑line Distance plus the horizontal ΔX and vertical ΔY components.
  4. To keep the measurement on the pattern, click 📌 Pin in that readout — it becomes a saved measurement annotation (and the note editor opens so you can label it). Otherwise, click two new points to measure again.
Straight measure doesn’t snap to lines, so for exact point‑to‑point work zoom in first, or use the guide ruler (below), which snaps to corners and edges.
Measuring the chest width. The green dashed line runs between your two clicks; the readout shows the distance plus the horizontal/vertical deltas, with Pin to keep it as a saved measurement.

Seam length & walk‑the‑seam

Use this whenever you need the length along a curved edge — an armhole, a neckline, a princess seam — rather than the straight distance across it. It’s also how you check that two edges which must sew together are the same length.

  1. Click 〰 Seam in the toolbar, or press S.
  2. Click a point on a pattern line — a highlight ring shows it locked onto that line. Then click a second point on the same line. The tool traces the edge between the two clicks and reports the length along the curve.
  3. To compare two edges, measure a second seam the same way. The readout now lists Seam A and Seam B with their lengths and the difference (Δ), flagged ✓ match or ✗ off against the tolerance — this is the “walk‑the‑seam” check.
  4. On a closed outline there are two ways around between your points; if it traced the long way, click ⇄ Flip last to take the other arc. Clear removes the seam measurements and starts over.
Both clicks must land on the same line entity. If the second click doesn’t register, you likely clicked a different line (e.g. the sew line instead of the cut line) — zoom in and click directly on the intended edge.
Walk‑the‑seam: the front armhole (Seam A) measured against the sleeve cap (Seam B). The readout compares the two curved lengths and flags ✓ match when they’re within tolerance.

The guide ruler

The guide ruler is a movable, rotatable ruler with tick marks — think of laying a physical ruler on the pattern. It’s a measuring aid; it isn’t saved unless you convert it to an annotation.

  1. Click ⇥ Ruler to drop one in the middle of the view. (Alternatively turn on 📏 Rulers to show the top/left ruler bars, then drag down from the top bar to pull out a guide.)
  2. It appears spanning the piece edge‑to‑edge with a 0 at the left end and tick marks along its length.
  3. Move it — drag the middle of the ruler to slide it around.
  4. Rotate / resize it — drag either square end. Hold Shift while dragging an end to snap the angle to 15° increments (handy for grain lines, 45° bias, right angles).
  5. Snap to the pattern — as you drag an end near a line, it locks onto the nearest corner or edge and shows a magenta marker, so you can measure exactly corner‑to‑corner.
  6. Keep or remove it — click the ruler to select it and get a small menu: 📌 Annotate turns it into a saved measurement that travels with the file; ✕ removes it. Double‑clicking a ruler also removes it.
The guide ruler laid across a piece. Ticks read from the 0 end; dragging an end near a corner snaps to it (magenta marker). Select the ruler to Annotate (save it) or remove it.

Show all measurements (🔢 Measures)

When you just want to see every measurement on a piece rather than measure by hand, use the 🔢 Measures toggle.

  1. Select a single piece in the sidebar (a specific piece at a specific size).
  2. Click 🔢 Measures in the toolbar. The tool automatically labels every boundary edge length (segmented at the corners, measured along curves), plus the overall width and height as dimension bars. Each measurement shows small start/end reference dots so you can see exactly which two points it spans.
  3. Any Points of measure you’ve defined on that piece are drawn too, resolved to the selected size with their values.
  4. Switch sizes or pieces and the labels update to that piece and size. Click 🔢 Measures again to turn the overlay off.
Want measurements on every piece at once? Pick a size chip (without selecting an individual piece) and turn on 🔢 Measures — the whole size is laid out on the artboard and each piece is dimensioned. With nothing selected the overlay stays off to avoid clutter.
Everything reads in the current unit — flip the Units selector to see the same piece in mm, cm or inches.

Positioning grid (⊞ Grid)

Click ⊞ Grid to lay a faint grid over the artboard. It’s handy for eyeballing spacing and lining pieces up while you rearrange them. The grid is in sync with the rulers — its lines sit on the same ticks, and it rescales as you zoom and when you switch units (mm · cm · inch). The origin (x=0, y=0) axes are drawn a touch brighter so you can see the datum. Grid works with or without the rulers showing.

Rearrange pieces on the artboard (✋ Move)

Use ✋ Move (X) to slide pieces around so you can compare them side by side — for example, butting a front and a back together to check that the armholes or side seams line up.

  1. Click ✋ Move. The view switches to show all pieces so you have something to align against.
  2. Drag any piece. As an edge, centre‑line or notch of the piece you’re dragging comes close to the same feature on a neighbour, a green dashed alignment guide appears and the piece snaps into line.
  3. Drop it where you want. To undo a placement, double‑click the piece and it jumps back to its original position (this also clears any rotation or flip).

Rotate & flip

Click a piece to select it (it gets a blue outline) and a small control floats just above its bounding box:

  • ⟲ / ⟳ — rotate the piece 90° counter‑clockwise / clockwise about its centre.
  • ⇄ / ⇅ — flip the piece horizontally (mirror left↔right) or vertically (mirror top↕bottom).

Rotating and flipping are useful for measuring or aligning a mirrored or off‑orientation piece against another. Like moves, they’re stored as a note in the DXF and reload with the file; double‑click the piece to reset everything.

Free transform

By default, dragging a piece snaps to the alignment guides. To position and rotate with no snapping at all:

  • Free move — click the 🧲 magnet in the floating control to turn snapping off (or just hold Alt while dragging). The piece follows the cursor exactly, with no guides.
  • Free rotate — drag the round handle that floats just above the selected piece to rotate it to any angle about its centre. Hold Shift to snap the angle to 15° steps.
While you’re in Move mode the pieces are drawn in a faint onion‑skin tint, so when one piece sits on top of another it stays see‑through and you can read the piece underneath. The piece you’re dragging is tinted a touch stronger so you can tell which one is on top. Overlapping tints blend, which makes it easy to see exactly where two edges or armholes line up.
Moving a piece never changes the pattern itself — the original coordinates are preserved. Your arrangement is stored as a note inside the DXF, so when you 💾 Save and reopen the file the layout comes back exactly as you left it.

Rename a piece (✎)

If the auto‑translated name isn’t what you call the piece, rename it: select the piece, then click the ✎ pencil next to its Name in the sidebar and type a new label. The name updates on the piece badge, the piece list, the nest view and every export. Renames are saved inside the DXF alongside your annotations.

Save a copy (💾 Save As…)

💾 Save DXF writes your annotations, renamed pieces and artboard layout back into the current file. 💾 Save As… instead downloads a separate copy under a new name, leaving the original untouched — useful for keeping a marked‑up or rearranged version next to the clean original.

Pair seams (🧵 Sew)

The 🧵 Sew tool lets you build a seam map — a record of which edge sews to which — by hand. It’s useful for checking that seams that join actually match in length, and it’s the foundation for the upcoming 3D sew‑up.

  1. Click 🧵 Sew. Each piece’s outline lights up, split into clickable edges at its corners and notches — so a long seam that meets two shorter edges can be joined notch‑to‑notch.
  2. Click an edge on one piece — a “thread” follows your cursor — then click the matching edge on another piece to pair them. Press Esc (or click empty space) to cancel a pick.
  3. Each seam gets a matching numbered tag and colour on both edges, a dashed connector between them, small direction arrows, and a length readout (e.g. 24.0 / 24.1 cm ✓, or a red ⚠ if the two edges differ by more than tolerance).
  4. The Seams panel lists every pair. Click one to highlight it, ⇄ flips the sew direction, and ✕ deletes it.
Seam maps are saved inside the DXF with 💾 Save DXF, so they travel with the file and reload where you left them. Pieces are matched by name, so pair seams on the size you’re working with.

3D sew preview (🪡 3D) — experimental

Once you’ve paired some seams, click 🪡 3D to see the pieces drape together in 3D. The tool wraps the pieces around a body form and stitches the seams you defined — so what you see comes straight from your seam map, not a guess.

  1. Pair the seams you care about with 🧵 Sew first, then click 🪡 3D.
  2. Drag to orbit the view and scroll to zoom.
  3. ⏸ / ▶ pauses or resumes the drape, ↺ Reset restarts it, 🧍 Body toggles the body form, and ▦ Wire shows the mesh instead of solid fabric.
  4. ✕ Close returns to the flat pattern.
This is an early preview meant for checking how pieces come together, not a precise fabric simulation. The more of the garment’s seams you pair, the more complete the drape.

5 Annotations

Annotations are notes you place on the pattern to flag issues, ask questions or approve details. There are four kinds — 📌 Point (a single pin), ↗ Arrow (a leader from an anchor to a target), ▭ Box (a region), and pinned measurements — and they’re saved inside the DXF, so they travel with the file to the factory and reload exactly where you left them.

Set your name first (once)

In the sidebar’s File section, type your name into the “Your name” box. Every annotation you create from then on is stamped with your name and the date. You only need to do this once per browser.

Create an annotation

  1. Pick a tool: 📌 Point (P), ↗ Arrow (L) or ▭ Box (B).
  2. Click on the pattern:
    • Point — one click drops the pin.
    • Arrow — click the anchor point, then click the target the arrow should point to.
    • Box — click one corner, then the opposite corner.
  3. The note editor opens automatically. Type your note, choose a category (Note, Issue, To‑do, Question, Approved) and a color from the palette, then click Save. (Press Esc to cancel a note you just started.)
  4. The annotation is auto‑tagged with the nearest piece, plus your name and today’s date.
The three note types on a piece — a numbered point (1), an arrow leader (2) and a dashed box region (3) — with the editor open: note text, category (Issue) and colour, stamped with piece, author and date.

Turn a measurement into a note

Any measurement can become a saved annotation: with the 📏 Measure tool, click two points and press 📌 Pin in the readout; or select a guide ruler and choose 📌 Annotate. Pinned measurements show their distance and behave like any other annotation.

Read, edit & delete

  1. Read — hover any pin to see its note in a tooltip.
  2. Edit — switch to ✥ Pan mode and click the pin; the editor reopens. Change the text, category or color and Save.
  3. Jump to one — in the Annotations panel (sidebar), click a row to center the view on that note and open it.
  4. Delete — click the ✕ on its row in the Annotations panel, or open it and press Delete. Made a mistake? Ctrl/Cmd+Z undoes it.

Filter & export the list

  1. In the Annotations panel, use the category and piece dropdowns to narrow the list — e.g. show only Issues, or only notes on the Front.
  2. Click ⬇ CSV to export exactly what’s shown. The spreadsheet includes the note, category, original and translated piece name, any measurement, author and date — handy for sending a factory just its open issues.
Notes support any language (including Chinese). Because they’re written into the DXF on 💾 Save DXF, a colleague who opens the same file sees your notes in place — no side files to pass around.

6 Grade nest & grade vectors

“Grading” is how a pattern scales from size to size. The nest view stacks every size of a piece on top of each other so you can see the progression, and grade vectors show the direction each landmark point grows. For the most accurate results, load the .rul grade‑rule file alongside the DXF (it sets the size order and which size is the sample).

View the size nest

  1. Click ▤ Nest in the toolbar, or press G. The canvas redraws with every size overlaid and colored by size, and the sidebar switches to a list of base pieces (one row per piece, e.g. “Front — 5 sizes”) with a color legend.
  2. Click a base piece in the list to isolate just that piece’s nested sizes — much easier to read than the whole pattern at once.
  3. The Selected piece panel shows a grade table: each size’s width × height and the ΔW (width change) versus the sample size, with the sample marked by an asterisk.
  4. Click ▤ Nest again to leave nest view and return to the normal per‑piece list.
Grade nest — every size of a piece overlaid and coloured by size, with the sample (M) drawn heavier. The sidebar shows the matching size legend.

Show grade vectors

  1. With a piece in view, click ↗ Grade.
  2. At each grade point the tool draws a small fan connecting that point across the sizes, with an arrow pointing toward the largest size — so you can see at a glance which way, and how much, each landmark moves as the pattern grows.
  3. Pieces that don’t grade (a pocket, a small trim) correctly show no vectors — a quick sanity check that fixed parts aren’t accidentally scaling.
  4. Click ↗ Grade again to hide the vectors.
Without a .rul the tool still groups sizes it can infer from the piece names, but the size order and sample size may not match your intent. Loading the .rul makes the nest, grade table, grade‑rules export and POM grading exact.

7 Quality checks & exports

This section covers the tools that verify a pattern is correct — seam allowance, symmetry, the automatic pattern check and points of measure — and the ways to get the results out (image, PDF report, Excel spec, 1:1 print).

Seam allowance (⌷ SA)

Seam allowance is the gap between the cut line and the sew line. This check highlights where that gap is inconsistent — often a sign of a digitizing slip.

  1. Click a single piece in the sidebar to select it (the check works one piece at a time and needs a piece that has both a cut line and a sew line).
  2. Click ⌷ SA in the toolbar.
  3. The sew line is dotted along its length: green dots where the allowance matches the typical value, red dots where it drifts more than 1 mm.
  4. The Selected piece panel reports the typical allowance, the min / max, and how many points are off — so you can tell an intentional stepped allowance (e.g. a wide hem) from an error.
  5. Click ⌷ SA again to turn the overlay off.

Symmetry & mirror pairs

  • Symmetry. The Selected piece panel shows how far a piece deviates from its own vertical mirror — “symmetric ±x” (e.g. a waistband) or “asymmetric (x)”. A quick check for pieces that should be cut on the fold.
  • Mirror pairs. The File panel notes when it detects left/right mirror‑pair pieces (matched by size, dimensions and mirrored overlay).

Pattern check (✔ QC)

Click ✔ QC to run an automatic audit of the whole file. Findings appear in a panel, sorted with errors first and warnings after; click any finding to zoom straight to the piece (and the exact spot for geometry issues). Re‑run with ↻ or export the list with ⬇.

  • Errors — no cut line, cut line not closed, cut line self‑intersects, a graded set missing one or more sizes, or notch/drill counts that differ between sizes of the same piece.
  • Warnings — duplicate / zero‑length points on the cut line, a missing grain line, or a mirror pair whose notch counts don’t match.
The QC panel lists findings by severity — errors first, then warnings. Clicking a finding zooms to the piece and the exact spot (shown here by the red locator ring).
A clean file reads “✓ All checks passed.” QC results are also included in the PDF report and as a tab in the Spec workbook, so a factory gets the same audit you see.

Points of measure (📐 POM)

A point of measure is a measurement you define once — a chest width, an armhole length, a total waist — that the tool then calculates automatically for every size and flags if a size grades unevenly. This is the heart of a graded spec.

Click 📐 POM to open the points‑of‑measure panel (top‑left). Define a measure by choosing one of three types:

  1. ＋ P–P (point‑to‑point) — for widths and lengths. Click the button, then click two points on the pattern. Each click snaps to the nearest vertex or edge (a magenta marker confirms the lock‑on). After the second point the name/tolerance box opens.
  2. ＋ Seam — for a length that follows a curve (armhole, neckline). Click the button, then click two points on the same line; the tool measures along the edge between them.
  3. ＋ Girth — for a value made of several parts (e.g. total waist = front + back). Click the button, then add segments one at a time: switch each segment between P–P and Seam, click its points, and repeat. The running segment count shows in the panel. Click ✓ Finish when all segments are added.

Then:

  1. Name it and set a tolerance. Give the measure a name (e.g. “Chest width”) and a grade tolerance (±). The tolerance is entered in your current display units — the field label follows the unit selector. Click Save.
  2. Read it across sizes. Click the measure in the list to expand it. You’ll see its value for every size; a size is flagged when its grade step differs from the others by more than the tolerance — catching a mis‑graded size before it ships.
  3. Jump to a size. Click any size box in the expanded view to isolate and zoom to that size’s piece, with the measurement drawn on it.
  4. Edit or remove. Re‑select to change the name/tolerance; click the ✕ on a measure to delete it.
A point‑to‑point Chest width defined once (left, snapped to two vertices) is calculated for every size in the panel (right). The XL box is flagged amber because its grade step exceeds the ±0.3 tolerance.

Where they’re saved. Points of measure are written into the DXF when you press 💾 Save DXF, so they reload automatically the next time you open the file (the tab shows a • until you save). Use ⬇ Export .pomset / ⬆ Import .pomset only when you want to copy the same set of measures onto a different style.

Exports

Several ways to get work out of the tool, each a single click in the toolbar:

  • 🖼 PNG — saves a snapshot of exactly what’s on screen as an image file. Frame the view how you want it first (zoom, pan, layers), then click.
  • 📄 PDF report — opens a printable report in a new tab containing the view snapshot, the QC results, the graded points‑of‑measure chart, and a table of every annotation (type, note, category, piece with English translation, measurement, author, date). In the print dialog choose “Save as PDF” as the destination. If nothing opens, allow pop‑ups for the site.
  • 📊 Spec (.xlsx) — downloads an Excel workbook with separate mm / cm / inch tabs; each lists every piece × size with width, height, perimeter, area and the deltas vs the sample size, plus a translated‑name column. It also adds a Grade rules tab (when a .rul is loaded), a Points of measure tab, a QC report tab, and a Marker (yield) tab.
  • 🧮 Yield — a fabric marker & yield estimate: choose a fabric width and scope (one size, or one of each size), and it nests the pieces and reports the marker length, fabric used (m / yd) and utilisation %, with a visual marker layout you can Save as PDF. Great for costing. It’s a bounding‑box estimate; a professional marker interlocks pieces and will use a bit less fabric.
  • 📋 Tech Pack — one consolidated document assembling the pattern snapshot, piece list (with materials, notches, drills), materials summary, graded POM chart, QC results, fabric yield, and annotations — ready to Save as PDF and hand to a factory.
  • 💾 Save DXF — writes your annotations and points of measure back into the DXF file (see §9).

Printing at actual size (🖨 1:1)

Use this to print a piece full‑size on paper — for a physical check or to cut a test.

  1. Frame the piece(s) you want (isolate a piece, or show all). Click 🖨 1:1. A new tab opens with a cover page followed by the pattern split into tiles across US Letter pages.
  2. Print that tab. Crucially, set scale to 100% / “Actual size” and turn OFF “Fit to page” or any scaling — otherwise the print won’t be true size.
  3. On the printed cover page, measure the 50 mm calibration bar with a ruler to confirm it’s exactly 50 mm. If it’s off, your print scaling is still on.
  4. Trim each tile to its border and tape them together using the R#·C# (row·column) labels to line them up.

8 Install as an app & open .dxf directly

You can run the DXF Tool like a normal desktop application and even make it the default program for .dxf files, so a double‑click opens the pattern instantly. This uses your browser’s built‑in app install and works in Chrome or Edge.

Install it

  1. Visit the site once in Chrome or Edge and sign in.
  2. Click ⤓ Install in the toolbar when it appears, or click the small install icon on the right of the browser’s address bar.
  3. Confirm the prompt. The tool now has its own window and icon, and opens without browser tabs and toolbars.

Open .dxf files by double‑click

  1. After installing, right‑click any .dxf file on your computer and choose Open with → Choose another app (Windows) or Get Info → Open with (Mac).
  2. Pick the installed DXF Tool and set it as the default.
  3. From then on, double‑clicking a .dxf opens it straight in the tool — each file in its own tab.
Install and “open directly” require the hosted https site (they don’t work when the page is opened from a local file). Safari and Firefox can use every feature of the tool, but don’t support installing it or registering it as the .dxf file handler.

9 Saving, tabs & comparing revisions

Saving your work

  1. Whenever a file has changes that aren’t saved yet — new annotations, edited notes, added points of measure — its tab shows a • dot.
  2. Click 💾 Save DXF. Your annotations and points of measure are written into the DXF itself.
  3. If you opened the file with 📂 Open (in Chrome/Edge) or via the file handler, the tool saves in place — the first time it asks for one‑time permission to edit that file; click Allow. Otherwise it downloads an annotated copy alongside your original.
Everything that gets baked into the DXF — notes in any language, categories, authors, dates and points of measure — reloads automatically the next time anyone opens that file, so the pattern is self‑contained.

Auto‑save

Turn on the 💾 Auto‑save switch in the sidebar’s File section to have the tool write your changes to the DXF automatically, a moment after you stop editing — no need to press Save DXF.

  1. Open the file with 📂 Open (or by double‑click if you’ve set the tool as the .dxf handler) in Chrome or Edge. This is what gives the tool permission to write back to the actual file.
  2. Tick 💾 Auto‑save. The first save may ask for one‑time permission to edit the file — click Allow.
  3. Edit as normal. The status next to the switch shows “Unsaved changes…” then “Saved 3:42 PM” after each automatic save. Your preference is remembered for next time.
Auto‑save writes in place, so it only works for files opened via 📂 Open in Chrome/Edge. If a file was dragged in, or you’re in Safari/Firefox, the switch shows “Open via 📂 Open (Chrome/Edge) to auto‑save” and you should use 💾 Save DXF (which downloads a copy) instead.

Tabs & unsaved changes

  • Switch between open files by clicking their tabs; add another with +.
  • Closing a tab that has unsaved changes prompts you to Save, Save As… (download a separate copy), Discard, or Cancel — so you never lose work by accident.
  • Undo / redo (Ctrl/Cmd+Z and Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+Z) is multi‑level and independent per tab.

Compare two revisions

  1. Open both versions of the pattern (each in its own tab).
  2. With one version active, open the Compare dropdown in the sidebar’s File panel and pick the other version.
  3. The other revision’s matching pieces overlay the current view in dashed magenta, aligned by centroid, with a per‑piece Δ deviation label (“= match” when within tolerance) — a fast way to see exactly what moved between versions.
  4. Click ⇄ Diff (next to the Compare dropdown) for a written “what changed” report: pieces added, pieces removed, and a table of changed pieces with each one’s max deviation and width/height change. Save it as PDF for a revision record.
  5. Set the dropdown back to Compare: none to clear the overlay.

11 Tips & tricks

Measuring like a patternmaker

  • Walk the seam. Use 〰 Seam to measure a curved edge length, then measure the edge it sews to — the readout flags whether they match. Catch mismatches before they hit the sample room or the 3D drape.
  • Snap corner‑to‑corner. With the guide ruler (⇥ Ruler), drag an end near a pattern line — it locks onto the exact corner/edge (magenta marker).
  • Lock an angle. Hold Shift while dragging a ruler end to snap to 15° steps (great for straight grain, 45° bias, right angles).
  • Keep a measurement. Select a ruler → 📌 Annotate to turn it into a saved dimension that travels with the file.

Working across sizes

  • Load the .rul with the .dxf so size order, sample size and the grade table are exact.
  • Isolate before you nest. Turn on ▤ Nest and click a single base piece for a clean, uncluttered size overlay; add ↗ Grade to see growth direction at each grade point.
  • Check the pockets. Pieces that don’t grade show no grade vectors — a quick sanity check.

Reviewing & sharing

  • Tag every note. Set your name once (sidebar → “Your name”); annotations are stamped with author, date and the nearest piece.
  • Colour‑code by intent. Use categories (Issue = red, To‑do = blue, Approved = green…) so a factory can scan the pattern at a glance.
  • Compare styles in tabs. Open several files; a • on a tab means unsaved changes. Use the Compare dropdown to overlay two revisions and read the Δ per piece.
  • Filter your notes. On a busy pattern, filter the Annotations list by category (e.g. only Issues) or piece, then export just those with ⬇ CSV.
  • Export the right thing: PNG for a quick image, PDF for a report with the full annotation list, Spec (.xlsx) for measurements across every size (mm/cm/inch tabs + grade rules), 🖨 1:1 to print a piece at actual size for a physical check.

Getting around

  • All tools always fit. On smaller screens the toolbar wraps onto a second row — nothing is hidden. The Units selector (mm · cm · inch) lives at the right of the toolbar.
  • Undo freely. Ctrl/Cmd+Z is multi‑level and per‑tab, so experiment without worry.
  • Turn on 🌐 EN for English piece names; hover any name to see the original.
  • Install it (Chrome/Edge) for a dedicated window and to open .dxf files by double‑click.

12 Notes & limits

  • Guide rulers are measuring aids only — they aren’t saved into the DXF (convert one to an annotation to keep it).
  • The English translation covers common garment terms; unusual terms stay in their original characters (hover to see the original).
  • Seam‑allowance check needs a piece that has both a cut line and a sew line.
  • The tool reads AAMA/ASTM DXF; it’s tuned to these exports and may need adjustment for other CAD systems.
